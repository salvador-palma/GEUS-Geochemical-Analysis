\begin{abstract}
Greenland is among the world's most mineral-rich but least-explored regions, and the
Geological Survey of Denmark and Greenland (GEUS) publishes decades of geochemical sampling
through open web services. This project asks what machine learning can recover from that
archive, and focuses on one question in depth: given a rock or sediment sample with some
elements measured, can a held-out element be predicted, and does knowing \emph{where} the
sample came from help?

We build a single pipeline and run it over two independent GEUS datasets --- whole-rock
geochemistry (6{,}957 samples, clustered along the West Greenland margin) and stream sediment
(17{,}463 samples, spanning the whole ice-free coast). A sample's own chemistry predicts a
held-out element well, reaching a mean \Rsq{} of 0.868 on stream sediment and 0.798 on whole
rock across 71 and 48 target elements respectively. Spatial context adds remarkably little on
top of it: nearest-neighbour chemistry, three full-coverage geophysical grids, and raw
coordinates each contribute less than one fold standard deviation, and are largely
interchangeable with one another. A graph neural network with learned, distance-weighted
aggregation does not beat a hand-specified average over the same neighbourhood.

The gain is not, however, uniform. Stratifying targets by how well own chemistry already
predicts them shows a clean monotone decay --- roughly $+0.05$ \Rsq{} for elements chemistry
predicts poorly, and nothing at all for elements it already predicts above 0.95 --- and this
curve reproduces almost exactly across the two datasets despite their different sampling
geometries and target sets. The elements that benefit are a coherent group: the hydrothermal
pathfinder suite (As, Sb, Pb, Cs on both datasets, plus U on stream sediment, which rises from
0.594 to 0.814). We also report a methodological result we think matters more than any single
number: an earlier version of this analysis, scored on each dataset's ten best-covered
elements, appeared to show a strong contrast between the two sampling geometries. Widening the
target set to a coverage threshold dissolved that contrast entirely, and 65\,\% of the apparent
effect turned out to be a single element. Target selection, not model choice, was the
first-order term.

All results use random $k$-fold cross-validation. Spatially blocked evaluation, which is where
these conclusions are most likely to change, is set out as the next stage of the work.
\end{abstract}

\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

Mineral exploration in Greenland has produced a large but fragmented public record. Since the
1950s, government surveys and exploration companies have sampled rock outcrops and stream
catchments along the ice-free margin, and the resulting analyses --- tens of thousands of
geochemical measurements, of which \num{46226} are used here --- are published by the
Geological Survey of Denmark and Greenland (GEUS) through open web services and the Greenland
Mineral Resources Portal \parencite{greenland_portal}.

The archive is attractive to machine learning for the same reasons it is awkward. Coverage is
heterogeneous: some outcrops carry dozens of samples while entire regions carry none. Analytical
suites differ by campaign, so the element matrix is sparse and its sparsity is not random.
Measurements are compositional and span six orders of magnitude, from weight percent to parts
per billion. And everything is spatially autocorrelated, because neighbouring catchments drain
the same lithologies \parencite{tobler1970}.

\section{Research questions}

Following the project agreement with the supervisor, the work was organised around three
questions:

\begin{description}
    \item[RQ0 --- report parsing.] Use a public large language model to convert scanned
        exploration reports into structured records. \emph{Dropped by agreement with the
        supervisor:} the scanned reports rarely contain the assay tables that would make
        parsing useful downstream, and the geochemical database is already available in
        structured form.
    \item[RQ1 --- missing geochemistry.] Given a sample with some elements measured, predict a
        held-out element from (a) the sample's other elements and (b) its spatial
        neighbourhood. \emph{This report.}
    \item[RQ2 --- unexplored regions.] Predict concentrations in a geological area held out
        \emph{spatially} from training, rather than at random. \emph{Scoped and prepared; see
        \Cref{ch:conclusion}.}
\end{description}

RQ2 as stated was initially ambiguous --- ``predict areas where no reports are available''
could mean predicting concentrations on unsampled ground, or predicting which regions are
prospective for mineralisation. The supervisor's clarification settles it as the first:
selecting a geological area outside the one used for training and predicting the numbers there.
This is a regression problem under spatial hold-out, not occurrence classification.

\section{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item A single preprocessing and evaluation pipeline applied unchanged to \emph{two}
        independent GEUS datasets with markedly different sampling geometries, allowing every
        claim to be checked for dataset-specificity rather than asserted from one.

    \item A systematic comparison of four ways of encoding a spatial neighbourhood as tabular
        features, across seven neighbourhood sizes. The result is unambiguous and holds on both
        datasets and under two model families: a neighbourhood should be \emph{pooled}, not
        \emph{enumerated}.

    \item Evidence that spatial context --- whether supplied as neighbouring chemistry,
        geophysical grids, or raw coordinates --- is largely redundant with a sample's own
        chemistry, and that the residual value is concentrated in a geologically coherent set
        of mobile and pathfinder elements.

    \item A graph neural network evaluated against the hand-built encodings on identical folds,
        including the specific question of whether edge distance carries information that
        fixed-radius averaging cannot. It does not.

    \item A methodological caution (\Cref{sec:correction}): scoring on the $n$ best-covered
        elements, a common convenience choice, manufactured an apparent effect that vanished
        under a principled coverage threshold.
\end{enumerate}

All experiments are reproducible from three notebooks against files committed to the
repository; every reported number is cached as JSON alongside the code.

\chapter{Background}
\label{ch:background}

\section{Geochemical data}

Geochemical analyses are \emph{compositional}: concentrations are parts of a whole and are
constrained to sum to a constant, which makes their covariance structure inherently negative
and their raw values ill-suited to methods assuming unbounded, symmetric errors
\parencite{aitchison1982}. The practical consequences for this work are two. First,
concentrations spanning weight percent to parts per billion are log-transformed before
modelling, which is standard practice and makes errors comparable across elements of very
different abundance. Second, elements are strongly co-determined --- a sample rich in
\ce{SiO2} is necessarily poor in something else --- which is precisely why predicting a
held-out element from its companions is feasible at all, and why it sets a demanding baseline
for any spatial method to beat.

A second feature of survey geochemistry is the \emph{detection limit}. An analysis that finds
nothing reports not zero but ``below detection'', often encoded as a negative or sentinel
value. Treating such entries as numbers rather than as missing data biases every downstream
statistic, so they are masked before any aggregation (\Cref{sec:pipeline}).

\section{Spatial structure and its evaluation}

Geochemical samples are spatially autocorrelated: nearby samples resemble each other because
they derive from the same rocks \parencite{tobler1970}. This is simultaneously the motivation
for spatial modelling --- a neighbour's chemistry is informative --- and the principal threat
to its evaluation. Under random cross-validation, a test sample frequently has training samples
a few hundred metres away, so a model can appear to generalise spatially when it is in fact
interpolating between near-duplicates. \Textcite{roberts2017} survey this failure mode and
recommend blocking folds in space so that test and training data are separated by a distance
comparable to the autocorrelation range.

\textbf{This report uses random $k$-fold cross-validation throughout, and its results should be
read accordingly.} Spatial blocking is the subject of RQ2 and is deliberately not conflated
with the present results; \Cref{sec:limitations} states precisely which conclusions are
expected to be sensitive to it. Two design choices partially mitigate leakage even here:
neighbour features are constructed from training coordinates only, and co-located samples can
be excluded from a sample's neighbourhood so that ``another sample from the same outcrop'' is
never mistaken for genuine spatial generalisation.

\section{Models for tabular and graph-structured data}

Gradient-boosted decision trees remain the strongest general-purpose learners on tabular data
of this size and heterogeneity, typically outperforming neural architectures without extensive
tuning \parencite{grinsztajn2022}. We use the histogram-based gradient boosting implementation
in scikit-learn \parencite{pedregosa2011}, which follows the binning strategy introduced by
LightGBM \parencite{ke2017lightgbm}: features are discretised before splits are searched, which
makes training near-linear in sample count and handles missing values natively --- an important property here, since the element matrix is
sparse by construction. Random forests \parencite{breiman2001} and XGBoost
\parencite{chen2016xgboost} are included as comparison points.

Graph neural networks offer a different way to use a neighbourhood: rather than summarising it
into fixed columns, they learn an aggregation function over a graph. \textsc{GraphSAGE}
\parencite{hamilton2017} aggregates neighbour representations with a permutation-invariant
operator and is distance-blind by construction. Graph attention networks
\parencite{velickovic2018} instead learn per-edge weights, and admit edge features --- which
allows the network to learn how quickly a neighbour's relevance decays with distance, something
a fixed average over the nearest $k$ samples cannot express. That contrast is the reason both
architectures appear here. Implementations use PyTorch Geometric \parencite{fey2019}.

Stream sediment's 523 raw element columns are 93 elements each measured by up to four

a detailed campaign --- and is therefore retained in both datasets, with its effect measured
separately (\Cref{sec:neighbours}).

Each source file carries a schema in JSON naming the sample identifier, metadata, physical and
location columns; element columns are whatever remains. Adding a dataset therefore requires a
schema file and three constants, not a code change. Longitude and latitude are classified as
metadata, so coordinates never enter the chemistry feature set by accident.

\section{Preprocessing}
\label{sec:pipeline}

The pipeline is six steps, and their order is load-bearing:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Mask below-detection values.} Entries $\leq 0$ encode ``below detection
        limit'' and become missing. This is done \emph{first}, so that a negative reading can
        never contaminate a median computed later.
    \item \textbf{Merge analytical columns.} Per-laboratory, per-method columns of the same
        element are collapsed into one by taking the median across whichever methods measured
        that sample. Methods differ in detection limit and bias, so the median is a deliberate
        choice of the neutral option rather than an obvious one. Stream sediment goes from 523
        columns to 93; whole rock is untouched.
    \item \textbf{Merge by sample number.} The median across duplicate rows describing one
        physical sample.
    \item \textbf{Log-transform.} Base-10 logarithm, applied after both merges, so the result
        is a log of medians rather than a median of logs.
    \item \textbf{Coverage filter.} Element columns measured in fewer than 10 samples are
        dropped. This is last on purpose: filtering earlier would discard measurements the two
        merges would have rescued.
    \item \textbf{Splits.} An 80/10/10 holdout and a five-fold random cross-validation
        assignment, both written to disk so every experiment sees identical folds.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Target selection}
\label{sec:targets}

An experiment of this shape requires a choice of which elements to predict. The obvious
convenience choice --- the $n$ best-covered elements --- turns out to be consequential, and
\Cref{sec:correction} reports what went wrong with it. All headline results in this report use
a \emph{coverage threshold} instead: every element measured in at least \num{1000} samples
qualifies as a target, giving 48 elements on whole rock and 71 on stream sediment. The
threshold is principled in a way a top-$n$ cut is not, because it does not depend on how many
elements a dataset happens to contain.

\section{Neighbour features}
\label{sec:neighbours}

Neighbour features are constructed at training time, per fold, and never cached to disk. A
$k$-d tree is fitted on \emph{training coordinates only} and queried with all rows, so a test
sample can never see another test sample. Self-matches are pushed to infinite distance. An
optional flag additionally discards distance-zero neighbours, which separates ``another sample
from the same outcrop'' from ``a genuinely different place''; all results below use it.

Four encodings of the resulting neighbourhood are compared. With $k$ neighbours and $m$
elements, they differ in how much they aggregate (\Cref{tab:encodings}).

\section{Geophysical features}
\label{sec:geophysics}

Geochemical neighbours only exist where somebody has already sampled; geophysical grids are
measured everywhere. They are therefore the natural substitute for a neighbourhood on
unexplored ground, and their inclusion answers the supervisor's request for a geologically
motivated input rather than a purely geometric one.

Three grids were included, on the criterion of \emph{complete Greenland coverage} assessed over
the whole landmass rather than over the samples at hand: magnetic anomaly
\parencite{geus_greenmag}, geothermal heat flow \parencite{geus_heatflow}, and depth to Moho
\parencite{geus_moho}. All three are valid at 100\,\% of samples. Magnetic anomaly tracks how
magnetite-rich the underlying rock is; heat flow reflects tectonic setting and the decay of
U, Th and K; Moho depth is crustal thickness, thin under rifted margins and thick under old
craton.

Several candidate products were rejected, and the reason is worth recording because it is not
visible from the files themselves. North Atlantic gravity compilations contain no missing
values over Greenland --- but only because they contain no \emph{measurement points} over
western Greenland, at a median distance of \SI{157}{\kilo\metre} from a sample to the nearest
grid node. Absence of \texttt{NaN} is not evidence of coverage.

\section{Models}
\label{sec:models}

Three tabular learners were compared on identical folds before fixing one for the remaining
experiments: a random forest \parencite{breiman2001} with 200 trees, histogram-based gradient
boosting \parencite{pedregosa2011} with 200 iterations, and XGBoost
\parencite{chen2016xgboost} with 200 estimators and the \texttt{hist} tree method. Results are
in \Cref{sec:modelchoice}. Gradient boosting was fastest and most accurate and is used
throughout; the random forest is retained as an independent check that conclusions are not an
artefact of one inductive bias.

\section{Graph neural network}
\label{sec:gnnmethod}

The graph has one node per sample and directed edges from each of a sample's $k$ nearest
\emph{training} neighbours, using exactly the mapping the tabular encodings use, so the network
sees the same neighbourhood the hand-built columns saw. Each edge carries $\log(1 + d)$ with
$d$ in kilometres as its feature.

Node features are the sample's own elements with the target removed. Because a network cannot
consume a missing value, each element contributes two channels: the standardised value, zeroed
where absent, and a binary present/absent flag, so the model can distinguish ``average'' from
``not measured''. Standardisation statistics come from training rows only.

Two architectures are compared, and the contrast is the experiment. \textsc{GraphSAGE}
\parencite{hamilton2017} aggregates neighbours without reference to distance. A graph attention
network \parencite{velickovic2018} with \texttt{edge\_dim=1} computes attention weights per
edge \emph{from the distance}, which is the one thing variants A--D structurally cannot encode.
Both use two message-passing layers of hidden width 64, a skip connection carrying the node's
own features directly to a two-layer head, dropout 0.1, and Adam at learning rate $10^{-2}$
with weight decay $10^{-4}$.

Training is full-batch and transductive: the whole graph is passed forward each epoch, but the
loss only ever sees training nodes. A validation slice is carved out of the \emph{training}
nodes to select the stopping epoch, so test data is never inspected during fitting. Runs were
executed on a Colab T4 GPU at approximately \SI{9.1}{\second} per fit at $k=20$.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

Every experiment reports the coefficient of determination \Rsq{} on the log-transformed scale,
per target element, averaged over five random folds; tables report the mean over target
elements. Only samples where the target was actually measured are scored.

Because a mean over dozens of elements hides a great deal, we also report the standard
deviation across folds, averaged over elements, as a noise floor. It is \num{0.0180} on whole
rock and \num{0.0137} on stream sediment. Differences smaller than this are not interpreted as
real, and are reported in units of it where the comparison matters.

\chapter{Results}
\label{ch:results}

\section{Own chemistry is a strong predictor}
\label{sec:baseline}

Predicting a held-out element from a sample's remaining elements reaches a mean \Rsq{} of
\num{0.7983} on whole rock (48 targets) and \num{0.8676} on stream sediment (71 targets). This
is a demanding baseline, and it is the number every spatial method in this report must improve
upon.

The two datasets are not directly comparable on this figure, because their target sets differ:
whole rock's 48 elements include a higher proportion of trace metals, stream sediment's 71
include a large suite of rare earth elements that are near-collinear with one another and
consequently easy to predict. Comparisons in this chapter are therefore always
\emph{within} a dataset.

\section{How many neighbours?}
\label{sec:ksweep}

\Cref{fig:ksweep} sweeps $k$ from 1 to 100. Two trends run in opposite directions, and both
appear on both datasets:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Variant~C improves with $k$ and then plateaus}, flattening around $k \approx
        20$--$50$. Because C averages over the neighbourhood, its width is 87 columns whatever
        $k$ is; larger $k$ therefore only smooths sampling noise and costs nothing.
    \item \textbf{Variant~D degrades monotonically with $k$}, and the decline tracks its column
        count exactly --- \num{1740} columns at $k=20$, \num{8700} at $k=100$. It contains
        strictly more information than C and uses it strictly worse.
\end{itemize}

The conclusion is the clearest in this report, and it holds across two datasets, two model

effectively free for variant~C, whose column count does not depend on $k$ (measured:
\SI{0.46}{\second} to construct at $k=20$ against \SI{0.48}{\second} at $k=10$).

\section{A correction: how target selection manufactured an effect}
\label{sec:correction}

An earlier version of this analysis scored both datasets on their ten best-covered elements.
Under that choice, variant~C was worth $+0.0102$ ($0.81\sigma$) on whole rock but $+0.0347$
($2.93\sigma$) on stream sediment --- a sharp contrast, which invited the reading that
\emph{sampling geometry} determines whether spatial context helps: dispersed sampling benefits,
clustered exploration sampling does not.

That contrast does not survive the coverage threshold. Under the principled target sets, whole
rock is essentially unchanged ($0.81\sigma \to 0.82\sigma$) while stream sediment collapses
from $2.93\sigma$ to $0.91\sigma$, leaving the two datasets statistically indistinguishable
(\Cref{tab:variants}).

The cause is a single element. Uranium's neighbour gain on stream sediment is $+0.2205$, an
order of magnitude larger than any other element's. In a ten-element mean it contributes
$+0.0220$ of the $+0.0342$ total --- \textbf{65\,\% of the headline was one element} --- while
in a 71-element mean it contributes $+0.0031$ of $+0.0125$, or 25\,\%. Excluding uranium
entirely, stream sediment's gain is $+0.0096$.

The two top-ten sets happened to differ in a way that exaggerated the contrast from both ends:
whole rock's is dominated by major oxides that own chemistry predicts easily, stream sediment's
is trace-heavy and, critically, contained the one element where neighbours genuinely pay. We
report this at length because the failure is invisible from inside the original experiment: the
numbers were correct, the folds were honest, and the conclusion was still an artefact of a
convenience choice made before any modelling.

The decay is monotone on both datasets and the two curves are nearly identical, despite the

own chemistry manages less than 0.70, it contributes around $+0.05$.

\textbf{This, rather than any pooled mean, is the central result of RQ1.} It also explains the
correction of \Cref{sec:correction} mechanically: changing the target set changes the mixture
of difficulty bands, and therefore the pooled average, without anything about the underlying
behaviour changing at all.

\paragraph{But it does not decide how much that information adds.} Five times the spatial signal
buys the same sub-$\sigma$ improvement on top of own chemistry. The reason is redundancy: a
sample's own chemistry already encodes most of what its location would tell you, because
chemistry is what location predicts.

\paragraph{The three spatial sources are interchangeable.} Geophysics, coordinates and

most likely to change under spatial cross-validation.

\paragraph{The hand-built encoding wins everywhere.} Variant~C is above both networks at all

\paragraph{Distance-aware attention does not pay, and eventually hurts.} The two architectures

margin that widens monotonically to $+0.0140$ at $k=100$. Run-to-run variation between repeats

outside it. The attention model also degrades past $k=20$ (\num{0.8205} to \num{0.8091}),
tracking edge count in exactly the way variant~D tracks column count.

\paragraph{On the widened target set the network matches the baseline, not the encoding.}
Running the settled configuration (\textsc{GraphSAGE}, $k=20$) on all 71 stream-sediment targets

else is worse than $-0.061$ (see \Cref{sec:limitations}). Excluding it, the network is worth
$+0.0037$ against variant~C's $+0.0128$, so it recovers roughly 29\,\% of the available gain
rather than none. Adding geophysical or coordinate channels to the node features moves the mean
by $+0.0004$ and $+0.0008$ respectively --- inside noise. On uranium the network exactly ties
the hand-built columns (\num{0.814} against \num{0.814}): it captures the single large spatial
signal and misses the many small ones.

\chapter{Discussion}
\label{ch:discussion}

\section{Spatial context is redundant, not absent}

The single sentence that summarises RQ1 is that \emph{a sample's own chemistry already contains
most of what its location could tell you}. Every route we tried into the spatial signal ---
averaging neighbouring samples, three independent geophysical fields, raw projected coordinates,
and a learned graph aggregation --- lands in the same narrow band of improvement, between
$+0.006$ and $+0.012$ mean \Rsq{}, all of it under one fold standard deviation.

That several very different encodings converge on the same small number is itself the argument.
If the limit were an encoding problem, one of them should have done materially better. The
consistent ceiling instead suggests the limit is informational: after conditioning on 48 or 71
co-measured elements, there is little left for position to explain.

This is not the same as saying spatial structure is absent. \Cref{sec:featuresets} shows a large
amount of it --- neighbours alone reach \Rsq{} \num{0.6086} on stream sediment with no access to
the sample's own chemistry whatsoever. The signal is real, substantial, and almost entirely
redundant with information the analyst already has.

\section{Why pooling beats enumeration}

Variants C and D contain the same measurements. C averages each element over the neighbourhood
and emits $m$ columns; D lists every element of every neighbour and emits $mk$. C improves with
$k$; D degrades with $k$, and the degradation tracks the column count on both datasets and in
the graph network's edge count as well.

The interpretation is that a neighbourhood carries a small amount of signal spread thinly across
many correlated measurements. Averaging concentrates it and suppresses per-sample noise at fixed
model capacity. Enumerating preserves the same signal but dilutes it across a feature space the
learner must now search, and the search cost exceeds the informational gain. Notably, the
neighbour ordering that variant~D preserves --- which sample was nearest --- turns out not to be
worth its dimensionality, which is consistent with the graph network's finding that edge
distance adds nothing once the neighbourhood is reasonably large.

\section{Why the graph network does not win}

The network was given every structural advantage: the same folds, the same neighbourhood, a
skip connection so the graph only had to add what it knew, and an architecture able to read edge
distance. It still does not beat an average.

Three non-exclusive explanations, in the order we find them plausible. First, with only element
concentrations as node features, the messages passed between neighbours carry little that a
per-element neighbour mean does not already summarise --- and gradient boosting is simply a
stronger tabular learner at this data scale \parencite{grinsztajn2022}. Second, the graph is
purely geometric: a $k$-nearest-neighbour graph in metres encodes proximity, whereas the
geologically meaningful adjacency is shared catchment, shared lithology or shared intrusive
suite, none of which is available to it. Third, the network is untuned beyond one hidden width
and one learning rate, so some margin certainly remains --- though not, we would argue, the
$+0.0125$ needed to overtake a 87-column average.

\section{The methodological result}

The finding we expect to be most useful to follow-up work on this archive is not about
neighbours at all. Scoring on the ten best-covered elements --- an entirely reasonable-looking
convenience --- produced a clean, interpretable, and false conclusion about sampling geometry
that survived model comparison, a $k$-sweep and a co-location control, because none of those
checks touch target selection. It was only visible after replacing the cut with a coverage
threshold.

The general lesson is that in a multi-target problem, the choice of which targets to score is a
modelling decision with the same status as the choice of features or folds, and it should be
justified rather than defaulted. It is worth adding that the artefact was not random: the
convenience cut favoured exactly the elements with the most data, which on stream sediment
included the one element where the effect under study is genuinely large.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Random cross-validation.} All results use random folds. Position is unusually
informative in this setting, because test samples frequently have training samples within a few
hundred metres. The reading most exposed to this is \Cref{sec:featuresets}'s conclusion that
geophysics does not beat coordinates: under spatial blocking, coordinates should degrade sharply
--- a model that has learned ``this northing implies this uranium level'' is useless when that
whole band is held out --- while the geophysical grids remain real measurements available at
every point. That comparison could invert, and testing it is the first task of RQ2.

\paragraph{Two derived target columns.} \texttt{Sum wt\%} in the stream-sediment dataset is
exactly the sum of the major oxides plus loss on ignition (median absolute difference
\SI{0.0000}{\percent} over \num{8482} samples, with 98.9\,\% within \SI{0.5}{\percent}). The
relation runs in both directions, so the major oxides are inflated by subtraction as well.
Separately, about ten elements appear twice under different unit conventions (\ce{Fe} as
\texttt{Fe \%} and \texttt{Fe2O3 wt\%}, $r=0.93$; \ce{Mn} likewise, $r=0.97$), which is
redundancy rather than leakage since the pairs come from different digestions. Both effects
raise absolute baselines and dilute measured gains. Neither affects any conclusion here, because
every claim is a \emph{difference} between two arms that see identical features --- but the
$>0.95$ band of \Cref{tab:difficulty} is partly populated by these columns and should not be
over-interpreted. The $<0.70$ band, which carries the finding, is unaffected.

\paragraph{Confounded explanation of the pathfinder result.} The elements that gain most are
mobile, hydrothermally mobilised elements --- and they are also the elements own chemistry
predicts worst. \Cref{sec:difficulty} shows gain tracks difficulty. Distinguishing ``spatial
context helps mobile elements'' from ``spatial context helps hard targets'' would require
comparing elements matched on baseline score, which this design does not do.

\paragraph{Scope of the model check.} The two-model comparison covers whole rock only
(\Cref{sec:modelindependence}). Five stream-sediment targets have fold standard deviations above
\num{0.03} (\ce{Ta}, \ce{Au}, \ce{U}, \ce{Pr}, \ce{Cl}) and gold is effectively unpredictable
from chemistry (\Rsq{} \num{0.128}). The graph network was tuned only lightly, and its
hyperparameters were not searched.

\chapter{Conclusion and future work}
\label{ch:conclusion}

We built one preprocessing and evaluation pipeline and applied it unchanged to two independent
GEUS geochemistry datasets in order to answer RQ1: whether a held-out element can be predicted
from a sample's other elements, and whether spatial context helps.

It can, and it largely does not. Own chemistry reaches a mean \Rsq{} of \num{0.868} on stream
sediment and \num{0.798} on whole rock across 71 and 48 target elements. Neighbouring chemistry,
geophysical grids and raw coordinates each add less than one fold standard deviation on top,
they are interchangeable with one another, and a graph neural network with learned
distance-weighted aggregation does not beat a plain average over the same neighbourhood. Where
the residual gain does land is systematic: roughly $+0.05$ for elements own chemistry predicts
poorly, nothing for elements it already predicts well, with the two datasets tracing almost the
same curve, and concentrated in a coherent set of pathfinder and mobile elements led by uranium.
Along the way we found that a conventional convenience --- scoring the ten best-covered elements
--- had manufactured an apparent dataset-level effect of which 65\,\% was a single element.

\section{Future work: RQ2}

The natural continuation, and the one the supervisor's framing asks for, is to hold out
\emph{regions} rather than samples:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Spatial block folds.} Hash projected coordinates into squares of side
        \texttt{blockKm} and assign whole blocks to folds, written in the same format as the
        existing fold files so no downstream code changes. Blocks are preferable to clustering,
        because block size in kilometres is an interpretable parameter whereas clusters follow
        sampling density \parencite{roberts2017}.
    \item \textbf{Re-run the core comparison.} Baseline, variant~C and the full feature-source
        ablation. This is the step that can change a conclusion: if geophysics overtakes
        coordinates under spatial hold-out, that is a substantive result and precisely the
        geologically motivated angle the project set out to add.
    \item \textbf{A block-size sweep.} Reporting \Rsq{} against block size gives a degradation
        curve --- the distance over which the model generalises --- which is a more useful
        deliverable to a geologist than any single number.
    \item \textbf{Geological province hold-out.} The stricter test: a coherent terrane rather
        than an arbitrary square, using the published subglacial province polygons.
\end{enumerate}

RQ2 will use stream sediment only. Whole rock spans \ang{11.7} of longitude within a single
region of West Greenland, so there is no meaningful ``outside'' to hold out, and block
cross-validation would either produce blocks too small to be spatially independent or fragment
the dataset. Its role in this project was as the control that made the sampling-geometry
comparison possible, and that role is complete.

Beyond RQ2, the most promising direction suggested by \Cref{sec:gnnresults} is not a better
network but a better graph. The present adjacency is purely geometric; encoding shared
catchment, mapped lithology or intrusive suite would give message passing something a distance
matrix cannot express, which is the condition under which a graph model could be expected to
overtake a well-chosen average.

Three notebooks, run in order:

The mean across-fold standard deviation of \Rsq{}, averaged over target elements, is
\num{0.0180} on whole rock and \num{0.0137} on stream sediment. Differences below this are not
interpreted as real anywhere in this report.
